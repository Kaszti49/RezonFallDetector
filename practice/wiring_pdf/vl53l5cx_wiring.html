Check before plugging in USB

  • Multimeter, continuity mode: ESP32 3V3 → sensor VIN beeps; ESP32 GND → sensor GND beeps; 3V3 → GND does NOT beep
  • SDA → 21 and SCL → 22, not swapped. Swapped is the most common mistake, and the scan then finds nothing
  • Robot battery disconnected; nothing else on 3V3/GND
  • After plugging in: the ESP32's red power LED is on and the sensor's LED (if it has one) is on. Touch the sensor: it must not get warm

How it works: from your hand to the screen

Every block below is something you just wired, or software that runs on it.

1 · Power

The USB cable gives 5 V. The regulator on the DevKit (AMS1117) turns it into 3.3 V for the ESP32 and, through the red wire, for the sensor. The sensor draws about 100 mA while measuring; the regulator can supply ~800 mA, so there's plenty of headroom. Black GND is the shared 0 V that all signals are measured against. Without it, no communication works.

2 · Measuring: time of flight

A tiny infrared laser (VCSEL, 940 nm, invisible, Class 1 eye-safe) flashes light across a 45° × 45° cone. A receiver grid of single-photon detectors (SPADs) is split into 8 × 8 = 64 zones. Each zone times how long the light took to come back:

distance = c · t / 2

c = 300 000 km/s, and /2 because the light goes there and back. A hand at 10 cm → t ≈ 0.67 ns. 1 m → 6.7 ns. The sensor builds a histogram of millions of photon arrivals, so it handles this timing itself. Each zone also gets a status: 5 or 9 = valid, anything else = ignore.

8×8 mode: 15 frames per second (one every ~66 ms). Range ≈ 2 cm to 4 m.

3 · Talking: the I²C bus

Two wires, shared. SCL = clock, always driven by the ESP32 (the “controller”). SDA = data, driven by whoever is talking. Both lines are open-drain: chips can only pull them down to 0 V. The pull-up resistors on the sensor board pull them back up to 3.3 V. That's why it's safe to share the bus and why the pull-ups must go to 3.3 V, not 5 V.

Every message starts with the sensor's address. The ESP32 says “0x29, I want to write”, and the sensor answers with an ACK by pulling SDA low on the 9th clock:

START = SDA falls while SCL is high. A bit is read while SCL is high. After the address come a 16-bit register number and the data bytes, then STOP.

The four control pins: LPn · I2C_RST · INT · PWREN

Power (VIN, GND) and data (SDA, SCL) are the obvious wires. These four carry no data. They decide whether the sensor listens, reset its I²C logic, tell the ESP32 that something happened, and switch its power.
Reading pin names: a trailing n (LPn), a bar over the name or a “#” means active-LOW: the function is ON when the pin is at 0 V. No “n” (I2C_RST, PWREN) means active-HIGH: ON at 3.3 V. “HIGH” for this sensor means above ≈ 0.7 × IOVDD (≈ 2.3 V); “LOW” means below ≈ 0.3 × IOVDD (≈ 1.0 V). Anything in between is undefined, which is why a floating (unconnected, no resistor) input is dangerous: it drifts through that zone with every bit of noise.

LPn: “Low-Power, active-low” = comms enable

What it is: an on/off switch for the sensor's I²C interface. ST calls it comms enable. HIGH: the chip listens to SDA/SCL. LOW: the chip ignores the bus completely, as if it weren't there. It stays powered, so its loaded firmware, its settings and its I²C address all survive. Bringing LPn back HIGH does not require the 84 KB upload again.

Electrically: a CMOS input referenced to IOVDD (3.3 V). It draws almost no current (µA), so an ESP32 GPIO drives it easily. Good breakouts add a pull-up resistor (often 47 kΩ) so it is HIGH by default; cheap ones sometimes don't. That's why we drive it from D18 instead of trusting the board.

Why it exists (the real reason): every VL53L5CX powers up at the same address, 0x29, and the address is not stored permanently. To put 2+ sensors on one I²C bus (e.g. front-left and front-right on the robot), give each sensor its own LPn wire:

  1. Hold every LPn LOW, so nobody answers.
  2. Raise sensor 1's LPn and tell it “your address is now 0x30”.
  3. Raise sensor 2's LPn: it's still 0x29, so tell it “0x31”. Repeat.
  4. Now all LPn are HIGH and each sensor answers at its own address.

In our sketch: pinMode(18, OUTPUT); digitalWrite(18, HIGH); runs before Wire.begin and stays HIGH.

Try it: with the page open, touch a jumper from D18's wire to GND (or change the code). The frames stop, because the ESP32's reads get no ACK. Release it and they come back.

I2C_RST: I²C interface reset

What it is: an active-HIGH reset for the sensor's I²C slave logic only, not the measuring core. Drive it 0 → 1 → 0 and the interface returns to “idle, waiting for START”.

Why you need it, the “stuck bus”: I²C has no timeouts. Suppose the ESP32 resets (you press EN, upload new code, or a motor causes a brown-out) while the sensor is sending a byte with SDA held LOW. The ESP32 reboots, but the sensor is still powered and still waiting for the clocks that would let it finish. SDA stays LOW forever. A new START needs SDA HIGH, so the bus is dead and the scan finds nothing until you unplug USB. A pulse on I2C_RST clears it instantly.

In our sketch: every boot does HIGH, 10 ms, LOW, 10 ms on D19, then holds it LOW. If the pin floated, noise could fire a reset mid-frame (garbage data or vanishing sensor). Boards usually have a pull-down; with the wire connected we don't rely on it.

INT: interrupt, “I have news”

What it is: the only control pin that is an output of the sensor. When a frame is finished (every ~66 ms at 15 Hz), the sensor pulls INT LOW for a short pulse.

Open-drain: inside the chip there is just a transistor between INT and GND. It can pull the line down but never drive it up. Something else must pull it up to HIGH: a resistor on the board, or the ESP32's internal ~45 kΩ pull-up, which we switch on with INPUT_PULLUP. Benefits: the HIGH level is whatever the pull-up goes to (3.3 V, perfect for the ESP32), and several sensors can share one INT wire (“wired-OR”: any of them can pull it low).

Why use it instead of asking: without INT the ESP32 must poll, i.e. keep asking “ready yet?” over I²C. Every question is a bus transaction, wasted time and wasted current. With INT the hardware tells the ESP32 the instant data exists: lower latency, a quiet bus, and the CPU can even sleep and be woken by INT (essential for battery devices).

On the ESP32: attachInterrupt(25, onSensorInt, FALLING). On every HIGH→LOW edge the CPU pauses whatever it's doing and runs the tiny ISR (interrupt service routine). It only adds 1 to a counter and sets a flag, in microseconds. Never do I²C inside an ISR (too slow, and not allowed). loop() sees the flag and reads the frame. If INT isn't working, a fallback poll every 20 ms keeps the demo alive.

Check it: the web page shows INT pulses/s. ≈ 15 means perfect. 0 with a heat-map still updating means the INT wire or pin is wrong and you are running on the polling fallback.

Why D25: it isn't a boot-strapping pin (0, 2, 5, 12, 15 are), it supports interrupts, and it has an internal pull-up (34–39 don't). Robot note: the robot plan used D25 for motor PWMA; that moves to another pin later.

Advanced: ST's driver has detection thresholds. The sensor only pulls INT when something is closer than X mm in chosen zones, so a robot or fall detector can sleep until something actually happens.

PWREN: power enable (board pin)

What it is: not a pin of the chip itself. It exists on some breakouts (generic 9-pin modules, ST's SATEL board) and is the EN input of the on-board regulator / load switch that feeds the chip's AVDD and IOVDD. HIGH = powered; LOW = completely off (µA).

PWREN vs LPn: LPn LOW is “stop listening, stay alive”. PWREN LOW is “power gone”: firmware, settings and address are lost, and the next start needs the full 84 KB upload (a few seconds) again.

Use: a hard reset if the sensor ever locks up, or zero-power standby in battery projects. Our build: tie it to 3V3 if your board has it. If you drive it from a GPIO instead, set it HIGH and wait ≥ 10 ms for the regulator before touching LPn or I²C.

AVDD vs IOVDD (on boards that expose both): AVDD = 3.3 V supply for the analog part and the laser driver. IOVDD = sets the logic level of SDA, SCL, LPn, I2C_RST and INT (1.8 V or 3.3 V). It must be 3.3 V to talk to an ESP32. Boards with a single VIN make both on-board.
